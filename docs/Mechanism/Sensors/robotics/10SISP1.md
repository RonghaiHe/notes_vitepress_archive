## SNR 
### Definition of SNR
What is a good sensor?
::: warning Definition: SNR
**SNR** (signal-to-noise ratio) : defined as the ratio of signal power to noise power, typically expressed in decibels (dB)
:::

$$
\mathrm{SNR} = \frac{P_\mathrm{signal}}{P_\mathrm{noise}} \quad \mathrm{SNR}(\mathrm{dB}) = 10\cdot\log_{10}\left(\frac{P_\mathrm{signal}}{P_\mathrm{noise}}\right).
$$

**Low Noise**!
- **Electronic noise** is a random fluctuation in an electrical signal, a characteristic of all electronic circuits.
- **Johnson–Nyquist noise** (thermal noise, Johnson noise, or Nyquist noise) is the electronic noise generated by the thermal agitation of the charge carriers (usually the electrons) inside an electrical conductor at equilibrium.
- Shot noise, flicker noise, burst/popcorn noise, Avalanche noise…

### Wheatstone bridge*
How to improve the SNR:
- Voltage divider: $V_\mathrm{out}=R/(R+R_T)V_\mathrm{DD}$
- **Wheatstone bridge**
  - $R_3$ is resistive sensor
  - $R_4$ is matched to nominal value of $R_3$
  - If $R_1=R_2$ and $R_4\approx R_3$ (nominal), $V_\mathrm{out-nominal}\approx 0$
  - $V_\mathrm{out}$ varies as $R_3$ changes

<figure>
   <img src="/Robotics/Sensors/robo10_1_bridge.JPG" alt="Wheatstone bridge" width="100%" align="center">
   <div align="center"><div align="center"><figcaption> Figure 1: Wheatstone bridge.</figcaption></div></div>
</figure>

For Wheatstone bridge:
Assume:

$$
R_1=R_2=R_0, \quad R_3=R_0(1+x+a), \quad R_4= R_0(1+a),
$$

where $x$: relative resistance change and $a$: common relative resistance drift. For the **Midpoint Voltage**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
  &V_L = \frac{R_4}{R_1+R_4}V_\mathrm{exc} = \frac{1+a}{2+a}V_\mathrm{exc} \\
  &V_R = \frac{R_3}{R_2+R_3}V_\mathrm{exc} = \frac{1+x+a}{2+x+a}V_\mathrm{exc}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Then:

$$
V_\mathrm{out}=V_R-V_L\approx\frac{x}{4}V_\mathrm{exc}
$$

Common temperature drift can be compensated!

If the bridge circuit is balanced: $V_\mathrm{out}=0, V_L=V_R$, then

$$
R_1R_3=R_2R_4
$$

## Signal process
### Fourier Transform

$$
F(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}f(t)e^{-j\omega t}\mathrm{~d}t=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}f(t)\cos\omega t\mathrm{~d}t - j\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}f(t)\sin\omega t\mathrm{~d}t
$$

### Analog low-pass filter
Impedance: Ohm’s law:

$$
V = IZ
$$

not $U = IR$ (**only for DC**)
- **Resistor**: $Z_R=R$
- **Capacitor**: $Z_C=\frac{1}{j\omega C}$; **low impedance** for high frequency
- **Inductor**: $Z_C=j\omega L$; **high impedance** for high frequency

::: warning Definition: Low-pass filters
In an **ideal** low-pass filter's, the frequency between pass and stop bands is called the cut-off frequency ($\omega_c$). All of the signals with frequencies below $\omega_c$ are transmitted and all other signals are stopped.
:::

<figure>
   <img src="/Robotics/Sensors/robo10_3_low-pass-filter.JPG" alt="Low-pass filter" width="100%" align="center">
   <div align="center"><div align="center"><figcaption> Figure 3: Low-pass filter.</figcaption></div></div>
</figure>

In **practical** filters, pass and stop bands are not clearly defined, $|H(j\omega)|$ varies continuously from its maximum toward zero.

The **cut-off frequency** is, therefore, defined as the frequency at which $|H(j\omega)|$ is reduced to  $1/\sqrt{2}\approx 0.7$ of its maximum value. This corresponds to signal power being reduced by $1/2$ as $P\propto V^2$.

#### RL filter
<figure>
   <img src="/Robotics/Sensors/robo10_4_RL.png" alt="RL filter" width="100%" align="center">
   <div align="center"><div align="center"><figcaption> Figure 4: RL filter.</figcaption></div></div>
</figure>

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_o&=\frac{R}{R+j \omega L} V_i \\
H(j \omega)&=\frac{V_o}{V_i}=\frac{R}{R+j \omega L}=\frac{1}{1+j(\omega L / R)}
\end{aligned}
$$

To find the cut-off frequency, we note

$$
\begin{aligned}
& |H(j \omega)|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(\omega L / R)^2}} \\
& |H(j \omega)|_{\max }=1 \quad \longrightarrow \quad\left|H\left(j \omega_c\right)\right|=|H(j \omega)|_{\omega=\omega_c}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|H(j \omega)|_{\max }=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\
& \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\left(\omega_c L / R\right)^2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \quad \longrightarrow \quad 1+\left(\frac{\omega_c L}{R}\right)^2=2 \\ 
\rightarrow&\omega_c=\frac{R}{L} \quad \text { and } \quad H(j \omega)=\frac{1}{1+j \omega / \omega_c}
\end{aligned}
$$

#### RC filter
<figure>
   <img src="/Robotics/Sensors/robo10_5_RC.JPG" alt="RC filter" width="100%" align="center">
   <div align="center"><div align="center"><figcaption> Figure 5: RC filter.</figcaption></div></div>
</figure>

$$
\begin{aligned}
& V_o=\frac{1 /(j \omega C)}{R+1 /(j \omega C)} V_i=\frac{1}{1+j(\omega R C)} V_i \\
& H(j \omega)=\frac{1}{1+j \omega R C}
\end{aligned}
$$

To find cut-off frequency (Similar to RL filter)

$$
\omega_c=\frac{1}{R C} \quad \text { and } \quad H(j \omega)=\frac{1}{1+j \omega / \omega_c}
$$

RL and RC filters above are part of the family of first-order filters (they include only one capacitor or inductor). In general, the voltage transfer function of a **first-order low-pass filter** is in the form:

$$
H(j\omega)=\frac{K}{1+j\omega/\omega_c}, \quad
\left\{
  \begin{aligned}
    &\text{For low-pass RL filters: } \omega_c=\frac{R}{L} \\
    &\text{For low-pass RC filters: } \omega_c=\frac{1}{RC} \\
  \end{aligned}\right.
$$

$$

### ADC
::: warning Definition: ADC
Analog-to-Digital converter (ADC, A/D, or A-to-D) is a system that converts an analog signal, such as a sound picked up by a microphone or light entering a digital camera, into a digital signal.
:::

An ADC may also provide an isolated measurement such as an electronic device that converts an input analog voltage or current to a digital number representing the magnitude of the voltage or current.

#### Example: LM35
Sensor output is a voltage (analog signal)

**LM35** is a low cost temperature sensor
- Basic Centigrade (2°C to 150°C)
- typical accuracy of 0.25°C at room temperature and 0.75°C for full range.
- output voltage has a **scale factor** of $10\mathrm{~mV}$ per degree

$$
\begin{aligned}
  &V_\mathrm{out}=10~(\mathrm{mV/^\circ C})\cdot \text{Temperature} \\
  &\mathrm{ADC_{result}} = 1024\cdot V_\mathrm{out} / V_\mathrm{ref}
\end{aligned}
$$

- 10-bit ADC: divide $V_\mathrm{ref}$ by $2^{10}$
- $0\sim V_\mathrm{ref}$ converted to $0\sim (2^{10}-1)$

When using $V_\mathrm{ref}=3.3\mathrm{~V}$, we have

$$
\text{Temperature} = \mathrm{ADC_{result}}\times 330 / 1024.
$$

### Sensor resolution
::: warning Definition: resolution
The resolution of a sensor is the **smallest change** it can detect in the quantity that it is measuring.
:::

The resolution of a sensor with a digital output is usually the resolution of the digital output.

The resolution is related to the **precision** with which the measurement is made, but they are not the same thing. A sensor's accuracy may be considerably worse than its resolution.

For example:

$$
\begin{aligned}
  &\text{Temperature} = \mathrm{ADC_{result}}\times 330 / 1024 \\
  &\textbf{Resolution: }  1\times 330 / 1024 \approx 0.32 \text{ degree}\\
\end{aligned}
$$

The **more bits** an ADC has, the **higher resolution** we can achieve

### Sampling
#### Definition
- In signal processing, **sampling** is the reduction of a continuous-time signal to a discrete-time signal. A common example is the conversion of a sound wave (a continuous signal) to a sequence of samples (a discrete-time signal).
- A sample is a value or set of values at a point in time and/or space. A sampler is a subsystem or operation that extracts samples from a continuous signal. A theoretical ideal sampler produces samples equivalent to the instantaneous value of the continuous signal at the desired points.
- For functions that vary with time, let $s(t)$ be a continuous function (or "signal") to be sampled, and let sampling be performed by measuring the value of the continuous function every $T$ seconds, which is called the **sampling interval** or the **sampling period**. Then the sampled function is given by the sequence:

$$
s(nT), \text{ for integar values of }n.
$$

- The sampling frequency or sampling rate, $f_s$, is the average number of samples obtained in one second (samples per second), thus $f_s = 1/T$.

#### Aliasing~
- Aliasing: Output signal due to sampling frequency of the sensor?
- An aliased signal provides a poor representation of the analog signal. Aliasing causes a **false lower frequency component** to appear in the sampled data of a signal.

<figure>
   <img src="/Robotics/Sensors/robo10_7_aliasing.JPG" alt="Aliasing" width="100%" align="center">
   <div align="center"><div align="center"><figcaption> Figure 7: Aliasing.</figcaption></div></div>
</figure>

When $x(t)$ is a function with a fourier transform $X(f)$:

$$
X(f)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}x(t)e^{-i2\pi ft}\mathrm{~d}t.
$$

The Poisson summation formula indicates that **the samples are sufficient to create a periodic summation** of $X(f)$

$$
X_s(f)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty X(f-kf_s)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty T\cdot x(nT)e^{-i2\pi nTf}
$$

which is a periodic function and its equivalent representation as a Fourier series. This function is also known as the discrete-time Fourier transform (DTFT) of the sample sequence.

Any frequency component above $f_s/2$ is s indistinguishable from a lower-frequency component, called an **alias**.

Two anti-aliasing solutions:
1. **Increase the sample rate** to cover all frequencies, i.e., $f_s > 2\cdot f_N$
2. Use **low pass filter**: When the sample-rate is pre-determined by other considerations (such as an industry standard), $x(t)$ is usually filtered to reduce its high frequencies to acceptable levels before it is sampled. The type of filter required is a lowpass filter, and in this application it is called an **anti-aliasing filter**.

#### Sampling Theorem
- The **Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem** serves as a fundamental bridge between continuous-time signals and discrete-time signals.
- It establishes a sufficient condition for a **sample rate** that permits a discrete sequence of samples to **capture all the information** from a continuous-time signal of finite bandwidth.
- Increasing the sampling frequency increases the number of data points acquired in a given time period.
- A fast sampling frequency provides a better representation of the original signal than a slower sampling frequency.
- For a given sampling frequency, the maximum frequency you can accurately represent without aliasing is the **Nyquist frequency**. The Nyquist frequency equals one-half the sampling frequency $f_N = f_s /2$ where $f_s$ is the sampling frequency.

#### R-2R ladder method
<figure>
   <img src="/Robotics/Sensors/robo10_10_explain_R-2R.png" alt="explain R-2R ladder method" width="100%" align="center">
   <div align="center"><div align="center"><figcaption> Figure 10: Explaination of R-2R ladder method.</figcaption></div></div>

$R_1$ and $R_2$ are connected in **parallel**, and this combination is in **series** with resistor $R_3$. The equivalent resistance of these three resistors can be calculated, which is called $R_A$:

$$
R_A = R_3 + \frac{R_1\times R_2}{R_1+R_2}=R+\frac{2R\times 2R}{2R+2R}=R+R=2R.
$$

<figure>
   <img src="/Robotics/Sensors/robo10_11_explain2_R-2R.png" alt="explain2 R-2R ladder method" width="100%" align="center">
   <div align="center"><div align="center"><figcaption> Figure 11: Explaination of R-2R ladder method.</figcaption></div></div>
</figure>

Similarly:

$$
R_B = R_5 + \frac{R_A\times R_4}{R_A+R_4}=R+\frac{2R\times 2R}{2R+2R}=R+R=2R.
$$

And finally, $R_D=R$, where $R_D$ is the resistor in the left.

- The op-amp input is at virtual ground
- Current into the op-amp stage:

$$
I = \frac{V_\mathrm{IN}}{R}
$$

- Output voltage:

$$
V_\mathrm{out}=-R_FI = -\frac{R_F}{R}V_\mathrm{IN}.
$$

   <img src="/Robotics/Sensors/robo10_12_thevenin.JPG" alt="explain thevenin" width="100%" align="center">
   <div align="center"><div align="center"><figcaption> Figure 12: Explaination of Thevenin equivalent voltage & resistance.</figcaption></div></div>

The two $2R$ resistors on the far left. One is connected to ground, the other to $V_A=+5\mathrm{~V}$.

- **Thevenin equivalent voltage** ($V_\mathrm{THA}$): These two $2R$ resistors form a voltage divider.

$$
V_\mathrm{THA} = V_A\times\frac{2R}{2R+2R}=\frac{5}{2}=2.5\mathrm{~V}.
$$

- **Thevenin equivalent resistance** ($R_\mathrm{THA}$): Set the voltage source to zero (short circuit to ground), the equivalent resistance seen from node A is two $2R$ resistors in parallel.

$$
R_\mathrm{THA} = 2R\parallel 2R = R.
$$

Similarly:

$$
\begin{aligned}
  &V_\mathrm{THB} = V_\mathrm{THA}\times\frac{2R}{2R+2R}=\frac{2.5}{2}=1.25\mathrm{~V}, \quad R_\mathrm{THB} = 2R\parallel 2R = R \\
  &V_\mathrm{THC} = V_\mathrm{THB}\times\frac{2R}{2R+2R}=\frac{1.25}{2}=0.625\mathrm{~V}, \quad R_\mathrm{THC} = 2R\parallel 2R = R \\
  &V_\mathrm{OUT} = V_\mathrm{THC}\times\frac{2R}{2R+2R}=\frac{0.625}{2}=0.3125\mathrm{~V}, \quad R_\mathrm{THOUT} = 2R\parallel 2R = R.
\end{aligned}
$$

#### PWM as DAC

PWM - **pulse width modulation**
- control the speed of DC motor
- control the brightness of LED

The **duty cycle** $D$ is defined as the ratio between the pulse duration ($\tau$) and the period ($T$) of a rectangular waveform:

$$
\text{Duty cycle }D = \frac{\tau}{T}.
$$

<figure>
   <img src="/Robotics/Sensors/robo10_14_diff-cycle.png" alt="diff-cycle" width="100%" align="center">
   <div align="center"><div align="center"><figcaption> Figure 14: Different duty cycle.</figcaption></div></div>
</figure>

Why PWM can work as DAC
- Desired DAC voltage = $A \times \text{duty cycle}$
- $A$ is the amplitude of the PWM (i.e., $A = 3.3\mathrm{~V}$ for LPC1743 ARM processor)

1. Fourier analysis of PWM as DAC voltage output: Assume we have a PWM: $\tau=5\mathrm{~\mu s},T=10\mathrm{~\mu s}\to D=50\%, A=3.3\mathrm{~V}$. 
frequency is $100 \mathrm{~kHz}$; the rise and fall times are both $10 \mathrm{~ns}$.

2. Use a low-pass filter: 
     - We want a stable $1.65 \mathrm{~V}$; don’t want the troublesome highfrequency spike (i.e., $100  \mathrm{~kHz}, 300 \mathrm{~kHz}$, …)
     - Use a low-pass filter in a PWM DAC: the filter retains the DC component while suppressing everything else.

<figure>
   <img src="/Robotics/Sensors/robo10_15_diff-cycle-f.JPG" alt="diff-cycle-f" width="100%" align="center">
   <div align="center"><div align="center"><figcaption> Figure 15: Different duty cycle in frequency domain.</figcaption></div></div>
</figure>

When $\omega_0=2\pi/T$:

$$
f(t)=DA+\sum_{n=1}^\infty A_n\cos(n\omega_0 t+\phi_n), \quad A_n=\frac{2A}{n\pi}|\sin(n\pi D)|.
$$

Set $n=1$, Only fundamental frequency considered:

$$
f(t)=DA+\frac{2A}{\pi}|\sin(\pi D)|\cos(\omega_0 t+\phi_1)
$$

- When $D=10\%$:

$$
f(t)=0.1A+0.197A\cos(\omega_0 t+\phi_1)
$$

- When $D=50\%$:

$$
f(t)=0.5A+0.637A\cos(\omega_0 t+\phi_1)
$$

where $0.5A$ is the DC component

- When $D=90\%$:

$$
f(t)=0.9A+0.197A\cos(\omega_0 t+\phi_1)
$$

Trade-off:
1. A lower cutoff frequency means **less ripple and longer settling time**; a higher cutoff frequency means more ripple and shorter settling time.
2. Higher-order filters improve performance, but they also **increase cost and complexity**. Instead of spending time and money implementing a fancy filter for a mediocre PWM DAC, we should just use an external DAC!

An Easy Way to Improve PWM DAC: just increase the frequency of the PWM signal!
- E.g., change $100 \mathrm{~kHz}$ to $10 \mathrm{~MHz}$
- Same RC filter with $f_c \approx 50 \mathrm{~kHz}$, the settling time is only about $15 \mathrm{~\mu s}$
